# 📘 Signals & Systems – Mathematical Leaning Syllabus

A rigorous syllabus that treats signals as mathematical objects (functions, distributions, elements of function spaces) rather than just engineering conventions. This builds strong foundations for Control Theory, Complex Analysis, and Applied Mathematics.

---

## 🔹 1. Mathematical Preliminaries

### Topics
- Functions as elements of vector spaces  
- \( L^p \) spaces: \( L^1, L^2 \)  
- Norms, inner products, orthogonality  
- Dirac delta and distributions (formal vs heuristic view)  
- Convolution defined rigorously in function spaces  

### References
- **Erwin Kreyszig – *Introductory Functional Analysis with Applications***  
  *Ch. 2: Normed spaces, inner products; Ch. 3: Hilbert spaces*  
- **Walter Rudin – *Principles of Mathematical Analysis***  
  *Ch. 6–8: Riemann–Stieltjes integral, sequences of functions, uniform convergence*  
- **Athanasios Papoulis – *Signals and Systems***  
  *Ch. 1: Mathematical description of signals; Ch. 2: System properties*  

---

## 🔹 2. Time–Domain Signal Analysis

### Topics
- Continuous and discrete signals  
- Linearity, time invariance, causality  
- Impulse/step responses (distributional interpretation)  
- Convolution: properties and examples  
- Stability: bounded-input bounded-output (BIBO)  

### References
- **Athanasios Papoulis – *Signals and Systems***  
  *Ch. 1–2: Fundamentals of signals and systems*  
- **B.P. Lathi – *Linear Systems and Signals***  
  *Ch. 1–2: Signals, linearity, convolution — clearer exposition than Oppenheim*  

---

## 🔹 3. Fourier Analysis

### Topics
- Fourier series: trigonometric and exponential forms  
- Orthogonality and basis functions  
- Fourier transform: definition, existence, properties  
- Parseval/Plancherel theorem (inner product view)  
- Applications: filtering, modulation, sampling  

### References
- **Athanasios Papoulis – *Signals and Systems***  
  *Ch. 3–4: Fourier series and Fourier transform*  
- **Elias Stein & Rami Shakarchi – *Fourier Analysis: An Introduction***  
  *Part I: Fourier series, convergence; Part II: Fourier transform, Schwartz space*  
- **R.N. Bracewell – *The Fourier Transform and Its Applications***  
  *Applied intuition and signal examples*  

---

## 🔹 4. Laplace and Z–Transforms

### Topics
- Laplace transform: definition, ROC (region of convergence)  
- Relation to Fourier transform  
- Transfer functions: poles, zeros, stability  
- Z–transform for discrete-time systems  

### References
- **Athanasios Papoulis – *Signals and Systems***  
  *Ch. 6–7: Laplace and Z–transforms*  
- **Doetsch – *Introduction to the Theory and Application of the Laplace Transform***  
  *Rigorous mathematical foundations for Laplace transform theory*  

---

## 🔹 5. Systems and Applications

### Topics
- Linear Time–Invariant (LTI) systems via convolution and transforms  
- Frequency response and resonance  
- Filters: low-pass, high-pass, band-pass  
- Sampling theorem (Nyquist–Shannon)  
- Hilbert transform and analytic signals  
- Connections to Control Theory (stability margins, frequency domain analysis)  

### References
- **Athanasios Papoulis – *Signals and Systems***  
  *Ch. 5–8: Systems, frequency response, applications*  
- **Simon Haykin – *Communication Systems***  
  *Introductory but precise treatment of modulation and signal transmission*  

---

## 📘 Guiding Principle

- Treat signals as **functions in vector spaces**, not just as piecewise notations.  
- Build Fourier and Laplace tools from **analysis** rather than engineering heuristics.  
- Use **distribution theory** to formalize impulses and steps.  
- Constantly connect results back to **Control Theory and Complex Analysis**.

---

## 📍 Outcome

By following this syllabus you will:
- Gain a rigorous understanding of signals as mathematical objects.  
- Master Fourier and Laplace analysis on a firm analytical foundation.  
- See direct bridges to **Complex Analysis** (residues, poles) and **Control Theory** (transfer functions, stability).  
- Align with the style of top-tier programs (Cambridge, MIT, ETH), where signals are taught from a math-first standpoint.
